The company catalogue

No applicant tracking system publishes a directory. Greenhouse has thousands of customers and no way to list them — /v1/boards and /v1/jobs both return 404, while /v1/boards/stripe/jobs returns 200. None of these platforms publishes a directory. The only way to reach a board is with a slug you already know.

That used to mean buying slugs one at a time: a web search per company, paid for by whoever wanted that field first. The catalogue reached 113 boards that way. Published lists then supplied the rest in two waves: Greenhouse, Lever, SmartRecruiters and Ashby brought it to roughly twelve thousand, and a second wave across the ten newer platforms — BambooHR the largest of them — added another eighteen thousand, taking the catalogue past thirty thousand employers with discovery paid for on none of them.

No list was taken on trust, and the vetting matched each list's measured risk. Clean lists were sample-probed through this app's own connectors — the same code that reads them in production. Lists that showed rot in sampling had every single row probed before being written down, which is how Jobvite's list went from "58% alive" in a twelve-board sample to 10 boards of 153 in the full pass: the sample had hit the one lucky cluster. Working out what each employer hires for

A slug alone is not enough. Sources are matched to a board by exact field, so an employer with no field assigned is in the table but reaches nobody. A list can supply the slug; it cannot say whether the company hires nurses or engineers.

Rather than pay a model to guess from company names, a background pass reads the jobs already on each board and takes the field with the most postings. A company name tells you nothing; thirty postings for veterinary technicians tell you everything. It costs one fetch and no tokens at all.

Reading real titles immediately exposed three faults that would otherwise have misfiled thousands of employers:

FaultMeasured on a live boardFix
Bare “engineer” read as software An HVAC manufacturer had 54 of 108 postings called software — field service, electrical, plant process Explicit match precedence, trades tested first. The pattern was not narrowed: somebody typing “engineer” into the box does mean software
No veterinary terms in care 285 of 297 postings on a veterinary board matched nothing at all Pattern widened; 286 of them now match
One posting could decide a board A film studio filed under marketing on a single vote out of seven A winner must clear two postings and 15% of the board, or the board is filed as other

Choosing which employers a board connects

A board connects 36 employers out of more than thirty thousand, so which 36 decides almost everything. They used to be chosen by field and by size, and neither says whether a company is hiring for the role somebody searched. Of eighteen employers on one programme-management board, eight were advertising programme-management roles — and none of those eight had been connected.

Classification already reads every title on every board, so it keeps the vocabulary it saw: up to 250 distinct words per employer. Selection then ranks candidates by how many of the searched-for words appear in what that employer actually posts, drawn from both the board's own field and the catalogue at large.

Two details decide whether that works. The words compared are the ones naming the role, not the whole sentence — "top tech company, 250k base salary minimum" put "tech", "company" and "base" into the comparison, which almost every employer matches, and that was worse than ordering by size. And the vocabulary keeps rare words, not frequent ones: at sixty words per employer it recorded each company's main line of work, so a company with ten programme-management roles among eight hundred postings recorded neither "technical" nor "programme".

What the sentence is allowed to decide

Criteria written as prose are read for a field, a salary floor, a seniority and a preference for remote work. Prose expresses a preference; the form expresses a rule. Writing "remote" ranks remote work first and admits every remote posting before any onsite one, while the checkbox — or writing "remote only" — excludes onsite work outright.

That distinction was got wrong once, at a cost worth recording. Reading a bare "remote" as a rule dropped 91 of the 108 matching postings on one board, five sixths of what the person had asked for, while the 250k salary floor they had also written dropped none at all.

Slow is not the same as gone

A board that times out used to be counted as a failure, and three failures retire an employer from every search on the service. The largest listings take longest to read — one returns three and a half megabytes and takes nearly thirty seconds — so the richest sources were the first to be deleted. Four of the twelve biggest employers in the catalogue had been retired this way before it was noticed, and a retired row was excluded from the query that would have re-read it, so nothing could bring it back.

Slowness and absence now have separate counters. A slow board is offered last rather than not at all, is read first when the background pass runs, and a successful read clears both. A board that answers with no openings counts as neither: an employer between hiring rounds has not gone away, and is simply looked at again in a few days.

Cheapest first, and paid discovery last. Each cron tick loads more of the published list, then classifies boards it already knows about, and only then considers paying to search for boards nobody has listed. Buying a slug through web search while an unclassified one sits in the table would be spending money on work already done, so discovery is blocked outright until that backlog is clear. When it does run it stops at a target per field, so the work is bounded and mostly happens once — and it is charged to its own budget rather than to whichever account happened to trigger the tick.

Where the API key is spent

Five call sites, nothing else. Every one is gated by the same daily allowance, and the allowance is claimed before the request goes out — so a crash mid-call costs the budget rather than escaping it. Company suggestions were the exception until recently: rate-limited per user but never counted, so they could be used to spend past a cap documented as the ceiling. They now reserve like everything else.

Ranking jobs

up to 4 calls per refresh

Reads your sentence and scores each posting against it. Jobs the built-in scorer already rates below 35 are never sent — paying to confirm a warehouse role isn’t a photography job is the easiest waste to cut. A refresh that finds nothing new costs nothing at all. Recency, pay and a stated preference for remote work are applied afterwards as arithmetic, because the model answers whether a posting matches and those are facts to read off the listing rather than judgements.

Finding employers

1 call per board, weekly

Decides which companies are worth watching, and verifies each has a live job board before connecting it. Blocked entirely while the catalogue still has unclassified boards, since those can be sorted out for free.

Suggesting companies

1 call, on request

Proposes employers resembling the ones already on a board. Low effort, because it is recall rather than analysis and somebody is waiting on it.

Reading your CV

1 call, on upload

Pulls out skills, titles and years. Works without a key too, just less well — the built-in reader handles the common case.

Scouting the web

~5–15¢ per run

The costly one: every search is billed on top of tokens. Finds organisations to approach directly, for work no job board carries. Opt-in, and off unless explicitly asked for.

Classifying employers

free

Not a model call at all. Reads the titles already on a board, counts them to decide the field, and keeps the words themselves so a later search can tell whether this employer advertises the role being looked for. Cheaper and more accurate than asking a model to guess from a company name — and it is why seeding thirty thousand employers costs nothing.

Nothing breaks without the key. Every path falls back to the built-in scorer, which is why a missing key was invisible for so long — the board kept working, just worse. That is also why the daily ceiling is enforced rather than advised: an overspend would have no symptom until the bill. Token counts are now recorded on every response and priced at published rates, so the figures at the top of this page are measured rather than inferred from payload caps — which is what they were until this was added.
